# --screen-- concept

One fabricated reference fails the project. One misquoted number embarrasses you at the defence.

That is why the group ran four checks on every source. The rule behind it is in Chapter A0: unverified AI fabrications result in immediate project failure.

# --screen-- concept

The four checks:

1. **The DOI opens the right paper.** Go to `https://doi.org/<DOI>`.
2. **Title, authors, year and journal match.** Search the DOI at search.crossref.org and compare every field.
3. **The paper says what we claim.** Read the abstract, and the full text for any number we quote.
4. **It's in a Consensus CSV.** Search the CSVs for the title.

# --screen-- mcq

## --question--

Which site checks a reference against the publisher's record?

## --options--

- [x] search.crossref.org
  > Yes. Crossref holds the publisher's record of each paper, so check 2 compares every field against it.
- [ ] Wikipedia
  > No. Wikipedia is not the publisher's record of a paper.
- [ ] ChatGPT
  > No. An AI assistant can write a reference that looks real. It doesn't check anything against the publisher's record.

## --why--

Check 2 searches the DOI at search.crossref.org and compares the title, authors, year and journal with the reference.

# --screen-- mcq

## --question--

A DOI opens a paper with a different title from the reference. What now?

## --options--

- [ ] Keep the reference; DOIs are often wrong
  > No. A DOI is a permanent address. If it opens a different paper, the reference does not describe a paper you have checked.
- [x] Fix the reference to match the real paper, or drop it if it's the wrong paper
  > Yes. Either the reference was copied wrongly, or it points at a paper that isn't the one you meant.
- [ ] Remove the DOI and keep the reference
  > No. Removing the DOI hides the problem. The reference would still point at a paper nobody verified.

## --why--

A source that fails check 1 or 2 is fixed to match the real paper, or dropped.

# --screen-- concept

**A number that meant something else.** The abstract of Adewuyi et al. (2026) says "complaint resolution time" fell by 83.33%.

The full text shows the figure measures the time to *lodge* a complaint: 30 minutes on foot to 5 minutes online. It is not the time to fix one.

Our paper says what the number really measures.

# --screen-- mcq

## --question--

Adewuyi et al.'s abstract reports a fall in "complaint resolution time". What does the full text show that figure measures?

## --options--

- [ ] Time to fix a complaint
  > No. That is what the abstract's wording suggests, and it is what the full text corrects.
- [x] Time to lodge a complaint
  > Yes. The figure is the fall from 30 minutes on foot to 5 minutes online to lodge a complaint.
- [ ] Time to get a room
  > No. The wait for a room is a separate result: 45 minutes down to 8.

## --why--

An abstract can name a number loosely. Only the full text shows what the number measures.
